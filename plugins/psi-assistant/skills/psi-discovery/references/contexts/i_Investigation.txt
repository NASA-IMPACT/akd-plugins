Comment[Approach]	Before being assembled into the ampoule, the samples were always ultrasonically cleaned in the sequence of (i) soapy water, (ii) water, (iii) 190 proof ethanol, and then dried in air or a compressed air. A double nitrogen purge was done before sealing the ampoule. Three configurations of the Al alloy filler and corresponding substrates were considered as relevant for the intended study: (i) HOLE, (ii) PIN and (iii) Hollow Pin. Solidification Using a Baffle in Sealed Ampoules” (SUBSA) furnace, which was designed for operation in the ISS’s Microgravity Science Glovebox (MSG) was used for this investigation. All samples were placed in the isothermal region of the furnace instrumented with thermocouples. The furnace was heated to the dwell temperature (600 - 605 °C ± 2K) at a rate of 5 K/minute (optimal 10K/minute). A dwell time was maintained for completion of capillary flow and establishing of the equilibrium meniscus shape. Subsequent cooling of the samples was performed at the maximum cooling rate allowed by the facility. Capillary topography identification and microstructure evaluation was performed on all samples.

Comment[Hypothesis]	A series of space (ISS) and terrestrial experiments, integrated with modeling and analysis are expected to answer the following practical and fundamental questions: (1) How can the absence of gravity and dominance of capillary forces be take advantage of in transporting the molten braze? (2) Which brazing alloy and the brazing technique will enable strong and efficient bonding of selected proxies of structural elements and facilitate a repair. That is, can brazing be done in space and/or on board of space vehicles by using flux-less braze? Specifically: A comparison between flux and flux-less brazing under space and terrestrial conditions can answer underlying scientific questions related to molten metal flow kinetics, specifically wetting and spreading of liquid clad/filler over topographically modified surfaces.

Comment[Objective]	The primary scientific goal of the proposed  terrestrial and planned microgravity molten metal  capillary flow experiments is to better understand the physics of the flow of molten metals over the topographically modified surfaces (e.g., filling in the holes, flow over sharp edges and/or flow over surfaces having variable curvatures). By performing capillary flow tests both on Earth and in a microgravity environment, the effects of microgravity on the kinetics of the larger masses of melt flow, and ultimate equilibrium shape of the meniscus will be closely examined. The objective of the microgravity experiments is to provide benchmark data against which theoretical models and computer simulations can be tested. The BRAINS project’s primary pragmatic/engineering obective is that a capillary flow of braze under microgravity conditions would enable a repair or bonding of larger and/or more irregular mating surfaces defects, compared to the terrestrial conditions. This would be important for future development of advanced brazing techniques with applications to both damage repair and construction in space.

Comment[Research Impacts]	A brazing process may be used to mitigate the consequences of high velocity micro meteoroids’ and/or space debris’ impacts, and possible catastrophic penetration of aluminum man-made objects in space. Second, construction in space would necessitate joining complex assemblies, e.g., habitat systems, with brazing being one meaningful manufacturing option of permanent bonding. Validation fundamental models including phase field models will allow prediction of terrestrial brazing quality.
